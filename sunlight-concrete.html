Sunlight Concrete

Apply the same three-question test to ready-mix and cement in northern Utah.

Current reality (public record)

  • Staker Parson (owned by CRH, Irish-owned) and Geneva Rock (Clyde Companies) dominate ready-mix in the Wasatch Front.
  • Concrete is perishable (about 90 minutes), so the delivery radius is the real market.
  • Two cement plants supply almost everything: Ash Grove (CRH) in Leamington and Amrize (formerly Holcim) at Devil’s Slide in Morgan County. Idaho has no cement plant, so entry is structurally hard.
  • During shortages, contractors and public projects absorb the delays and cost overruns while producers keep selling.

Minimum viable ledger

The smallest set of lines that makes this ledger useful. Each line is filled from a public source below.

  • Market share by ready-mix producer in Davis, Weber, Box Elder and Salt Lake counties.
  • Cement source and price.
  • Bid results on public projects (UDOT, cities, schools): who won, at what price, and who lost.
  • Delivery-radius coverage, and any exclusive contracts or preferred-vendor arrangements.
  • Ownership links and any cross-ownership or family or corporate ties, documenting only what is public.
  • Outcomes: project delays, change orders and cost overruns tied to supply issues.

Market-Power Test: Concrete

Three questions to ask of any company or sector. Each one is answered from public records, not opinion.

  1. Choice. Can a customer or taxpayer walk away and use a real alternative?
  2. Concentration. Do a few firms hold most of the market in this place?
  3. Public backing. Does public money, a public charter or a public guarantee stand behind it?

The more of these the record answers “no choice”, “a few firms” or “yes, public backing”, the more a full public ledger helps people see the whole picture.

Utah starting moves

  1. Pull a recent year of UDOT bid results and list who bid and who won on concrete paving and structures.
  2. Map the permitted gravel pits, quarries and concrete plants from Utah’s public air-quality approval orders and mine permits, by county.
  3. For one county, file one ledger page: the supply sites and their owners, the public bids and awards, and the state payments, each linked to its source.

First look · copied by hand from the published public figures; not pulled automatically.

Cement shipped to Utah, 2024: about 1.62 million metric tons of portland and blended cement (77,064 t portland plus 1,543,282 t blended), as reported by cement companies to the USGS. Source: USGS Mineral Industry Surveys, Cement in August 2025 (published December 2025), tables 2a and 2b, from the USGS cement statistics page.

This counts cement delivered to Utah customers, not cement made in Utah. The USGS groups Utah’s plants with other states for production figures, so a Utah-only production number is not published in this report.

Clinker made in Utah’s production group, 2024: about 3.38 million metric tons (3,378,012 t) of clinker, the main ingredient of cement, were produced in the USGS district that covers Idaho, Montana, Nevada, Oregon, Utah and Washington. That is about 4.7% of the 71.5 million metric tons produced nationwide. Source: USGS Mineral Industry Surveys, Cement in August 2025 (published December 2025), table 4, from the USGS cement statistics page.

This is a six-state total, not a Utah figure, and the USGS does not publish a split by state or by plant. It measures clinker, which is ground with other materials to make cement, so it is not the same as cement shipped. The USGS notes these figures are accurate to about three significant digits. A table of Utah public-project concrete bids was not added because the bid data could not be reliably reached and read for this sample.

Average U.S. cement price, 2025 (estimated): about $160 per metric ton, measured as the average mill unit value, unchanged from the 2024 estimate and up from $127 in 2021 and $152 in 2023. Source: USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 (published February 2026), Cement chapter, from the USGS cement summary.

This is a national average, not a Utah price, and the USGS does not publish a state-level price in this summary. It is the value at the plant, before delivery, so it is not what a contractor pays for cement or for ready-mixed concrete. The 2024 and 2025 figures are USGS estimates, rounded to two significant digits.

U.S. cement use and imports, 2025 (estimated): about 110 million metric tons of cement were used nationwide (apparent consumption), unchanged from 2024. Imports made up about 21% of that use (net import reliance), and about 23 million metric tons of hydraulic cement were imported. Source: USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 (published February 2026), Cement chapter, from the USGS cement summary.

“Apparent consumption” is worked out from production plus imports minus exports, adjusted for stock changes. It is not a count of cement actually poured. These are national figures, the 2025 values are USGS estimates rounded to two significant digits, and no Utah import share is published.

Where U.S. cement is made, 2025: cement was produced at 97 plants in 34 states and Puerto Rico. Texas, Missouri, California and Florida, the four leading producers, made about 44% of the U.S. total. Source: USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 (published February 2026), Cement chapter, from the USGS cement summary.

This counts plants, not owners. Several plants can belong to one company, so it does not show how many companies control supply. Utah is not named among the leading states, and the summary gives no Utah plant count.

U.S. cement shipments, 2025 (estimated): about 100 million metric tons shipped to final customers, with an estimated value of about $17 billion. An estimated 70% to 75% of sales went to ready-mixed concrete producers. Source: USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 (published February 2026), Cement chapter, from the USGS cement summary.

This is a national estimate rounded by the USGS, and its shipment total includes exports. It sits beside the Utah figure above, which counts about 1.62 million metric tons shipped to Utah in 2024, but the two figures come from different USGS reports and years, so they should not be divided into a precise Utah share.
